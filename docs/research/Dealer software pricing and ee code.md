# Give away compliance, sell hosting and servicing

The open-source DMS should give away everything a single-lot dealer needs to sell cars legally, and charge for the things dealer software buyers already expect to pay for. Those are managed hosting, buy-here-pay-here (BHPH) loan servicing, automation, extra locations and third-party data. Small-dealer software has settled on a **flat fee per dealership with unlimited users**. DealerCenter's **$99/month core grows to roughly $300–$500 once modules are added**, Frazer bundles BHPH and accounting for **$129 desktop or $199 hosted**, and one freemium rival (GARAGEBIRDS) gives away a hosted plan with no limits on cars or users. That points to this ladder. A **$0 self-hosted Community edition** (one location, unlimited users, all compliance, security and export). A **$49/month Hosted Core**. A **$99/month Hosted Pro**, which equals hosting plus a $50 Pro feature license that self-hosters can buy on its own. Each additional location costs a published **$25/month**. Third-party pulls are either bring-your-own-account or billed at a published per-unit price. The launch promotion should run about **six months to a fixed date announced on day one**, because that gives dealers time for several month-end closes and stays within the FTC "Free" guide's six-months-in-twelve norm. It should need **no card on file and no auto-conversion**, end with a downgrade rather than a lockout, and reward founding dealers with a written **24-month price lock**. Paid ee/ code should go in the **public repository under a commercial license**, gated by a signed license key, as Documenso, Formbricks, Twenty, PostHog and Metabase do. One founder cannot afford the cost of a separate private repo, which GitLab and Metabase both abandoned. Only billing, tenant operations, signing keys and partner-restricted integrations should stay private. Two conditions apply. The ee code must carry the commercial license from its first commit, even while it costs nothing. And a contributor license agreement (CLA) must be in place before the first outside pull request.

A note on evidence: most detailed price tables come from comparison pages written by competing vendors (DealerVLO, GARAGEBIRDS, DealersCloud, AutoRaptor). They usually cite each vendor's own page with a check date, and Frazer's prices were confirmed on its own page. No vendor publishes a second-location price. No survey of independent-dealer software spend or willingness to pay exists. The specific price points below are therefore recommendations reasoned from competitor anchors, not measured demand.

## Dealer software sells a ~$99 core, then charges per module and per deal

The independent-dealer market uses three pricing styles. **Modular** vendors publish a low core and charge for each module. DealerCenter lists the DMS at **$99/month**, with CRM Plus $99, CRM Pro $199, integrated accounting $99, BHPH $50 and a premium website $99, all with unlimited users ([DealersCloud cost guide](https://www.dealerscloud.com/resources/dealer-management-software-cost)). Its BHPH module includes 500 texts, and its QuickBooks integration costs $40 ([Capterra DealerCenter](https://www.capterra.com/p/71935/DealerCenter/pricing/)). Stacked with a website, feeds and BHPH, the bill reaches **$298/month**. With accounting and reconditioning added, it reaches **$496/month** ([DealerVLO](https://www.dealervlo.com/blog/best-dms-independent-used-car-dealers)). **Bundled** vendors compete by including exactly what DealerCenter charges for. Frazer costs **$129/month, $387/quarter or $1,299/year on desktop, or from $199/month hosted**, and that price covers BHPH, a full accounting suite, forms, retail installment contracts, installation, training and unlimited support. Its own pricing page compares this with an "Other DMS" that totals $240 once add-ons are stacked ([Frazer](https://www.frazer.com/pricing)). **Tiered** vendors split by deal type or seat count. Wayne Reaves charges **$75 for cash-only, $125 with bank finance and $145 with BHPH collections**, includes three users, and charges $10 per extra user ([GARAGEBIRDS](https://garagebirds.com/cheapest-dealer-management-software.html)). Lot Wizard runs from **$99 for one user to $199 for four to eight** ([Capterra Lot Wizard](https://www.capterra.com/p/13556/Lot-Wizard/pricing/)).

The low end is already very cheap. DealerVLO charges **$29/month** with unlimited users, title forms, BHPH schedules, a CRM, a website and listing feeds ([DealerVLO](https://www.dealervlo.com/blog/best-dms-independent-used-car-dealers)). GARAGEBIRDS offers a **free plan with no time limit, no card, unlimited users and no vehicle cap**, covering inventory, cash deals and 300+ forms. Its **$49–$59 Pro plan** adds finance desking, custom contract mapping, a website, marketplace posting and consignment ([GARAGEBIRDS](https://garagebirds.com/how-much-does-a-dms-cost.html)). Desk2Deal's free plan caps usage instead, at one user, 10 deals and 10 cars ([GARAGEBIRDS](https://garagebirds.com/how-much-does-a-dms-cost.html)).

### Every vendor meters third-party data per use

Variable cost sits in third-party services, and almost every vendor passes it through per transaction because the vendor pays for it too. DealerCenter charges **$3 per eContract and $10 per AutoCheck report**, and book values cost **$64–$74/month per guide** ([DealersCloud](https://www.dealerscloud.com/resources/dealer-management-software-cost)). Credit reports cost about **$4 per report plus $24.99/month per bureau** ([GARAGEBIRDS](https://garagebirds.com/cheapest-dealer-management-software.html)). One source quotes $3.40–$5.70 per report, which may reflect a different bureau or package ([AutoRaptor](https://www.autoraptor.com/blog/automotive-crm-pricing-compared-what-vinsolutions-elead-dealersocket-and-dealercenter-really-cost-in-2026)). California LAW 553 contract forms cost **$2.98–$3.50 per deal** ([GARAGEBIRDS](https://garagebirds.com/how-much-does-a-dms-cost.html)). NMVTIS reports run **$0.25 each** through DealerCenter ([rfp.wiki](https://www.rfp.wiki/specialty-industries/dealer-management-systems/frazer/dealercenter)). Carfax is the largest item. It is quote-only, and one dealer reported paying **$1,089/month** ([CarCheckerVIN](https://www.carcheckervin.com/carfax-dealer-pricing)). That alone can exceed the dealer's entire DMS bill.

### Unlimited users is standard, and no vendor publishes a second-location price

Users are effectively free. DealerCenter, Carsforsale, DealersCloud, DealerVLO and GARAGEBIRDS all price per dealership with unlimited users ([DealersCloud pricing](https://www.dealerscloud.com/pricing); [Capterra Carsforsale](https://www.capterra.com/p/178449/CarsForSale)). Reviewers complain that DealerCenter "becomes expensive as more team licenses are added" ([Capterra DealerCenter](https://www.capterra.com/p/71935/DealerCenter/pricing/)), so per-seat pricing would put a newcomer at a disadvantage. Locations are the opposite: nobody publishes the price. GARAGEBIRDS states plainly that "none of the vendors state a price for a second location" across ten DMS vendors ([GARAGEBIRDS](https://garagebirds.com/how-much-does-a-dms-cost.html)). Adjacent tools do publish one. Podium charges **+$500 per location**, and some CRMs price per rooftop ([CarSalesSoftware.com](https://carsalessoftware.com/dealership-software-pricing)). The "per dealership" wording suggests each extra lot is billed as another subscription, but that is inference. A clear, published multi-location price would therefore stand out.

Terms are buyer-friendly. Most small-dealer vendors charge **no setup fee**, run month-to-month, and give **16–20% off for annual prepay**: Frazer $1,299 against $1,548, GARAGEBIRDS $49 against $59, and Desk2Deal 20% ([Frazer](https://www.frazer.com/pricing); [GARAGEBIRDS](https://garagebirds.com/cheapest-dealer-management-software.html)). Trials run **14–30 days**, and DealerCenter's is the longest at 30 ([GARAGEBIRDS](https://garagebirds.com/how-much-does-a-dms-cost.html)). Dealers' complaints match those terms. DealerCenter reviewers object to "extra fees for certain features", "hidden charges" and "nickel-and-dime" add-ons. Dealer Car Search reviewers describe contracts that kept billing after a switch, a roughly $2,400 balance sent to collections, and a website shut off without notice ([Capterra DealerCenter](https://www.capterra.com/p/71935/DealerCenter/pricing/); [Capterra Dealer Car Search](https://www.capterra.com/p/169090/Dealer-Car-Search/)). The positioning follows directly: no contract, published prices, no per-seat fees, free export and no integration tax.

## A $0 / $49 / $99 ladder, with BHPH servicing as the paid anchor

### The free line follows the license, not a policy

The approved design already guarantees that compliance, security, one location and data export are always free. The strongest way to keep that promise is to put all of it in the **AGPL core**. Released AGPL code cannot be withdrawn; a relicense reaches only future releases, which is why HashiCorp's 2023 switch produced a fork of the last open version ([InfoQ](https://www.infoq.com/news/2023/08/hashicorp-adopts-bsl)). So no future paywall can take these features back, and dealers can verify that without trusting the founder. Security belongs on that list for a legal reason too. The FTC Safeguards Rule requires MFA for "any individual accessing any information system" and logging of authorized users' activity ([16 CFR 314.4](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-16/chapter-I/subchapter-C/part-314/section-314.4)). Other open-core products often gate these features: Formbricks sells 2FA in its enterprise tier, and Cal.com moved audit logging into closed code ([Formbricks](https://formbricks.com/docs/self-hosting/advanced/license); [Cal.diy](https://cal.com/blog/cal-diy-open-source-to-closed-source)). A DMS cannot follow that pattern.

Beyond the guaranteed items, the free tier should match what competitors treat as **table stakes**. That means inventory with VIN decoding, listing-feed export, deal jacket and forms, basic desking and payment calculation, customer and lead records, and a simple public inventory page. DealerCenter's $99 core and Carsforsale's $99 bundle include these, and GARAGEBIRDS gives them away ([Capterra DealerCenter](https://www.capterra.com/p/71935/DealerCenter/pricing/); [GARAGEBIRDS](https://garagebirds.com/how-much-does-a-dms-cost.html)). Paid features should sit where vendors already charge and where the feature makes or saves the dealer money: **BHPH servicing** (+$50 at DealerCenter, top tier only at Wayne Reaves), CRM automation (DealerCenter's $199 Pro adds workflows, campaigns and call recording), accounting sync, AI features and multi-location ([DealersCloud BHPH guide](https://www.dealerscloud.com/resources/best-buy-here-pay-here-software); [Capterra DealerCenter](https://www.capterra.com/p/71935/DealerCenter/pricing/)). BHPH is the natural anchor for Pro. BHPH and other in-house financing accounted for **32.6% of independent-dealer originations in 2025** ([NIADA](https://niada.com/dashboard/niadas-ucir-shows-independent-used-sales-top-9-8-million-in-2025/)). A BHPH lot also gets ongoing value from servicing (payment posting, collections, Metro 2 credit reporting), not just at the point of sale.

|---|---|---|
| Inventory, VIN decode, recon status, listing-feed export, public inventory page | Yes | Custom-domain website with lead forms and SEO (hosted) |

### Price points: hosting is the product, Pro is a fixed add-on

| Plan | Monthly | Annual (~17% off) | Contents |
|---|---|---|---|
| Community (self-host) | $0 | $0 | Everything in the free column; community support |
| Hosted Core | $49 | $490 | Community, managed: backups, uptime, automatic rule-pack and security updates, email support |
| Hosted Pro | $99 | $990 | Hosted Core plus all `ee/` features |
| Self-host Pro license | $50 | $500 | `ee/` features on the dealer's own server via license key |
| Additional location (any plan) | +$25 each | +$250 each | Second and later lots; unlocks multi-location features |
| Onboarding/data migration | Optional flat fee, waived on annual plans | — | Import from spreadsheets or another DMS |

The structure is easy to explain: Hosted Pro is **$49 of hosting plus $50 of Pro**, and the self-hoster buys only the $50. Each price has a reason. **$49 for hosting** sits at GARAGEBIRDS Pro's level and well under DealerCenter's $99 core. It is justified because a host carries the Safeguards burden and keeps every lot current. Plausible notes that security fixes reach cloud users immediately but reach self-hosters only after they upgrade, and the same lag applies to California rule changes ([Plausible](https://plausible.io/self-hosted-web-analytics)). **$99 for Pro** equals DealerCenter's core alone. That stack bundles what DealerCenter sells as DMS + BHPH + CRM + QuickBooks for about $388 before per-use fees ([Capterra DealerCenter](https://www.capterra.com/p/71935/DealerCenter/pricing/)). It also undercuts Frazer's $199 hosted bundle, which is built around the same BHPH-plus-accounting pitch ([Frazer](https://www.frazer.com/pricing)). Matching DealerVLO's $29 is unnecessary, because the depth of the compliance engine and records storage is the differentiator. A free hosted tier to match GARAGEBIRDS is also unnecessary: the self-hosted Community edition is the free tier, and hosting personal data for free creates Safeguards exposure with no revenue to cover it.

The design choices around the price points matter as much as the numbers. **Users stay unlimited on every plan**, because per-seat pricing is the complaint competitors already advertise against. **No usage caps on cars or deals.** Desk2Deal and FoxDMS gate on volume, but capability gating (GARAGEBIRDS) avoids penalizing a growing lot ([GARAGEBIRDS](https://garagebirds.com/how-much-does-a-dms-cost.html)). **Additional locations are a published flat fee**, not a new subscription, so a two-lot Pro dealer pays $124 rather than about $198 for two full plans. A second lot is the clearest signal that a dealer can pay, and publishing the price is a selling point because no one else does. **Third-party costs are never folded into the base price.** Every integration supports bring-your-own credentials free on every plan: the dealer's own bureau account, NMVTIS provider, Carfax or AutoCheck subscription, or Twilio number. Where the founder resells a service under a partner agreement, it is billed per unit at a price published next to the plan prices. The free compliance promise covers the *NMVTIS gate*, not the report, which still comes from the dealer's provider at about $0.25. **No contracts, no setup fee and no cancellation penalty**, with self-serve export available even after cancellation.

The strongest argument against this ladder is Frazer's. Dealers respond to one all-in price, and any split invites "nickel-and-dime" complaints. Two plans, a location add-on and pass-through data is close to the minimum structure that still separates cash lots from BHPH lots, the same split Wayne Reaves uses. Merging Hosted Core into Pro at about $79 is a defensible simplification if early dealers find two plans confusing. The opposite risk is that $49 is too high for a cash-only lot comparing against free GARAGEBIRDS or $29 DealerVLO. The launch promotion is the test: if founding cash lots will not pay $49 for hosting, lower Core rather than move compliance features into Pro.

## Run the promotion six months to a fixed date, then let dealers opt in

### Six months fits the dealer workflow and the FTC "Free" guide

A dealer cannot judge a DMS in a 14-day SaaS trial, the length used by 62% of B2B products ([ChartMogul](https://chartmogul.com/reports/saas-conversion-report/)). They need to load inventory, close real deals, and go through at least two or three month-end closes, and a BHPH lot needs to post several payment cycles. That argues for months, not weeks. The FTC's guide on the word "Free" sets the upper limit. Introductory free offers must in good faith end "after a limited time", after which the product is sold at its regular price. Repeated free promotions of the same product in a trade area should total **no more than six months in any 12-month period**, with no more than three offers at least 30 days apart ([16 CFR 251.1](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/16/251.1)). The guide is not binding law, but it sets the norm regulators look at. The recommendation is a **six-month promotion ending on a fixed calendar date** printed on the pricing page at launch, followed by no further "free" promotion for at least six months.

The promotion is in effect a long *reverse trial*: every feature is unlocked, then the account drops to the free tier. 7% of products use that model as their entry point ([ChartMogul](https://chartmogul.com/reports/saas-conversion-report/)). Plan for modest conversion. ChartMogul's 2026 median free-to-paid rate is **8%**, freemium converts at a "good" 3–5%, and card-required trials at 25–35% ([ChartMogul](https://chartmogul.com/reports/saas-conversion-report/)). No data exists on conversion from a free launch period, or for dealer software. The promotion should therefore be judged by how many founding dealers become paying references, not by the raw conversion rate. If hosting is offered during the promotion, cap the hosted founding cohort, for example at the first 25 dealers, to bound support load. The one sourced founding-dealer offer in this market limited it to the first 10 dealerships ([CarSalesSoftware.com](https://carsalessoftware.com/dealership-software-pricing)).

### End it the way Docker did, not the way Akaunting did

The paywall transitions in the research show a consistent pattern. Backlash grew with short notice, data loss, small users losing access, and anything that felt like a broken open-source promise. **Akaunting gave cloud users about seven days** to pick a $12/month plan and offered export only by contacting support ([Akaunting](https://akaunting.com/blog/akaunting-3-1-speed-licensing-and-plans)). **Heroku deleted free Postgres databases** about three months after its announcement ([Heroku FAQ](https://help.heroku.com/RSBRUH58/removal-of-heroku-free-product-plans-faq)). Unity's per-install fee led to a reversal a year later, in which its new CEO said the company "can't pursue that mission in conflict with our customers" and promised price changes at most once a year ([Unity](https://unity.com/blog/unity-is-canceling-the-runtime-fee)). Transitions that went more smoothly kept small users free and set long, fixed windows. Docker gave a **five-month grace period** and kept companies under 250 employees and $10M revenue free ([InfoQ](https://www.infoq.com/news/2021/09/docker-desktop-subscriptions)). Postman gave about four months and an in-app migration tool ([Postman](https://blog.postman.com/announcing-new-lightweight-postman-api-client/)). Slack gave 45 days but let customers renew early to lock the old price for a year ([TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2022/07/18/slack-is-increasing-prices-and-changing-the-way-its-free-plan-works/)).

Applied to the DMS, that pattern gives a concrete plan. On day one, publish the end date, the post-promotion prices and the always-free list, so the conditions are disclosed "clearly and conspicuously at the outset" as the FTC guide requires ([16 CFR 251.1](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/16/251.1)). Send in-app and email reminders at **90, 30 and 7 days** before the end. That exceeds Slack and matches Heroku and Postman. On the end date, accounts that have not chosen a plan **downgrade to Community**. Pro data becomes read-only and stays exportable, and nothing is deleted or locked. One carve-out protects dealers from real harm. BHPH loans originated during the promotion keep free **payment posting, payoff quotes and statements for the life of those loans**, while new BHPH originations and automation require Pro. Cutting off servicing of live customer loans would be the Heroku database-deletion mistake in dealer form. Deal-jacket retention and export are core features in any case, so the dealer's DMV and finance retention duties are never at risk.

Founding-dealer pricing should reward use, not mere signup. Dealers who record real activity during the promotion, such as a minimum number of deals, get **about 30% off any paid plan, locked in writing for 24 months**. For example, Hosted Pro at $69 instead of $99. The lock matches the one sourced offer in this market, which gave founding dealers a 24-month price lock ([CarSalesSoftware.com](https://carsalessoftware.com/dealership-software-pricing)). It also avoids the trap DealerVLO warns about, introductory rates that "reset higher" without warning ([DealerVLO cost blog](https://www.dealervlo.com/blog/dealer-software-cost-flat-fee-vs-per-user)). After the lock, apply Unity's rule: price changes take effect at most once a year and only at renewal, with at least 60 days' notice.

### California's ARL is triggered only if the promotion converts automatically

The cleanest legal design is **no payment method collected during the promotion and no automatic conversion**. At the end, the dealer actively chooses a plan, accepting updated terms by clickwrap and entering billing details. Without a card on file and an automatic charge, the promotion itself is not a negative-option offer. The recurring-billing rules then apply to the paid subscription the dealer chooses to start.

| Rule | Status (Nov 2026) | What it requires | Applies to this promotion? |
|---|---|---|---|
| California Automatic Renewal Law, Bus. & Prof. Code §17600 et seq. (AB 2863, eff. Jul 1 2025) | In force | Free-to-paid conversions expressly covered. Express affirmative consent, with records kept 3 years. Online click-to-cancel shown next to any retention offer. Notice 3–21 days before a free trial longer than 31 days ends. Price-change notice 7–30 days ahead. Annual reminder | Only with auto-conversion; drafted around consumers, so business buyers may fall outside (unverified) |
| ROSCA (federal) | In force; actively enforced | Clear disclosure of material terms before collecting billing info; express informed consent; simple cancellation | Applies to online recurring paid plans |
| FTC Click-to-Cancel Rule | Vacated by the Eighth Circuit, Jul 2025; ANPRM Mar 2026; no final rule | Would add federal click-to-cancel | Watch |
| FTC "Free" guide, 16 CFR 251 | Non-binding guidance | Conditions disclosed at the outset; introductory offers time-limited; ≤6 months of free offers per 12 | Yes |

Sources: [Fenwick](https://fenwick.com/insights/publications/california-tightens-requirements-for-automatically-renewing-subscriptions); [CA AG alert](https://oag.ca.gov/news/press-releases/attorney-general-bonta-issues-consumer-alert-california’s-automatic-renewal-law); [Jones Day](https://www.jonesday.com/en/insights/2026/05/ftc-revives-clicktocancel-rule-new-risks-for-subscription-businesses); [Morgan Lewis](https://www.morganlewis.com/pubs/2025/07/ftcs-click-to-cancel-rule-vacated-ahead-of-planned-july-14-effective-date); [16 CFR 251.1](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/16/251.1).

Two uncertainties remain. First, the ARL is framed around "consumer services, subscriptions, and plans" ([CA AG](https://oag.ca.gov/news/press-releases/attorney-general-bonta-issues-consumer-alert-california’s-automatic-renewal-law)). The statutory definition of "consumer" was not verified, so whether a used-car dealership, and especially a sole proprietor, is covered is unresolved. It has no private right of action but is increasingly cited in class actions ([Fenwick](https://fenwick.com/insights/publications/california-tightens-requirements-for-automatically-renewing-subscriptions)). About 30 states have auto-renewal laws, and ROSCA enforcement has produced settlements such as Amazon Prime's $2.5B ([Jones Day](https://www.jonesday.com/en/insights/2026/05/ftc-revives-clicktocancel-rule-new-risks-for-subscription-businesses)). The practical answer is to **build the paid plans to the ARL standard regardless**: express consent with stored records, online cancellation in the same place as signup, annual reminders and advance notice of price changes. The cost is a few screens and emails. Second, no sourced California authority was found on the notice needed to change the terms of a free service. The safe practice is advance notice plus fresh clickwrap assent to the paid terms, never a "terms may change at any time" clause, and counsel should review that point before the promotion ends.

## Publish ee code in the main repo; keep only hosted operations private

### The TypeScript peer group keeps paid code public under a commercial license

Among JavaScript and TypeScript products closest to this project, the norm is a **public ee directory in the same monorepo** under a "production use requires a subscription" license, unlocked by a key. Documenso keeps `packages/ee` under the Documenso Commercial License beside an AGPLv3 root ([Documenso ee/LICENSE](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/documenso/documenso/main/packages/ee/LICENSE)). Formbricks does the same in `apps/web/modules/ee`, with MIT client SDKs ([Formbricks LICENSE](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/formbricks/formbricks/main/LICENSE)). Twenty, an AGPL CRM in a TypeScript monorepo, marks commercial code **per file** with a `/* @license Enterprise */` header ([Twenty LICENSE](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/twentyhq/twenty/main/LICENSE)). PostHog, Rocket.Chat, Strapi, n8n and Langfuse use the same arrangement ([PostHog](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/PostHog/posthog/master/LICENSE); [n8n](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/n8n-io/n8n/master/LICENSE_EE.md); [Langfuse](https://langfuse.com/handbook/chapters/open-source)). Metabase ships one repo with an AGPL `metabase/metabase` build and a commercial `metabase-enterprise` build ([Metabase LICENSE](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/metabase/metabase/master/LICENSE.txt)).

The two best-documented moves both went from private to public, and both were made to cut operating cost. GitLab merged CE and EE in 2019 after a single security release needed **about 150 merge requests** across the two repos ([GitLab](https://about.gitlab.com/blog/2019/08/23/a-single-codebase-for-gitlab-community-and-enterprise-edition/)). Metabase found two repos "well over twice as much work" to merge and release, and changes to the open-source code broke Enterprise in ways that reached customers ([Metabase](https://www.metabase.com/blog/opening-metabase-enterprise)). Those were teams of dozens. For a solo founder building with Claude Code, two repos means two CI pipelines, sync merges and drift, and that overhead weighs relatively more.

The counterexamples are real but do not fit this situation. **Grafana** ships a proprietary Enterprise binary and refuses to make it source-available. **Mattermost** keeps a public source-available folder plus a private peer repo wired in by build tags ([Grafana](https://grafana.com/blog/2021/04/20/qa-with-our-ceo-on-relicensing/); [Mattermost README](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/mattermost/mattermost/master/server/enterprise/README.md)). Both have dedicated build teams. **Cal.com** closed its production code in April 2026, saying AI tools make open code "like giving attackers the blueprints to the vault". It relicensed the stripped public repo from AGPL to MIT as Cal.diy and now backports security fixes between the two repos ([Cal.com](https://cal.com/blog/cal-com-goes-closed-source-why); [Cal.diy](https://cal.com/blog/cal-diy-open-source-to-closed-source)). Discourse publicly rejected that reasoning. It argued that obscurity is brittle, that frontend code reaches browsers anyway, and that closing code mainly reduces the number of defenders ([Discourse Meta](https://meta.discourse.org/t/discourse-is-not-going-closed-source/400863)). For a product whose pitch is open-source transparency to dealers wary of lock-in, copying Cal.com would undercut that pitch.

| Option | Who uses it | Gains | Costs |
|---|---|---|---|
| Public `ee/` + commercial license + key (recommended) | Documenso, Formbricks, Twenty, PostHog, Metabase, n8n, Langfuse | One repo and CI; auditable by dealers; contributors see the boundary | Checks are strippable; only the license protects ee code |
| Private repo or peer/submodule | Mattermost (hybrid); GitLab before 2019; Metabase before 2020 | Server-side ee hidden | ~2× merge/release work; drift; client code still exposed |
| Proprietary binary on open core | Grafana | Clean separation for distributed builds | Separate build pipeline |
| Closed production, stripped public edition | Cal.com (2026) | Maximum control | Trust damage, cross-repo backports, community-only fork |

### License keys enforce the line; secrecy does not

Every surveyed vendor enforces ee code with a **license key plus a legal license**, and none claims the key cannot be broken. Grafana uses a signed `license.jwt` that renews every 24 hours and degrades only enterprise features when it expires ([Grafana docs](https://grafana.com/docs/grafana/latest/administration/enterprise-licensing/)). Documenso validates its key at startup and caches it so a brief outage won't lock users out ([Documenso docs](https://docs.documenso.com/docs/self-hosting/configuration/license)). A licensing vendor's guidance, which favors keys and so is biased, covers the remaining design. Sign the license proof. Check entitlements on the server, not only in the UI. Model features as entitlements rather than editions. Deny only the paid operation on failure. Use a signed offline file for air-gapped installs. Avoid kill switches. Design for "the honest majority" ([PermitCore](https://dev.to/permitcore/open-core-in-a-public-repo-gate-paid-features-with-license-keys-not-secrecy-14g3)).

For this DMS, that means an **Ed25519-signed JWT** verified against a public key embedded in the build. It carries entitlements (`pro`, `locations: N`, `expires`) and optionally refreshes online with a grace period. The same mechanism runs the launch promotion. Every install, hosted or self-hosted, receives a free signed key whose `expires` value is the published end date. At expiry, entitlements fall back to Community automatically, and the read-only and BHPH-servicing carve-outs above apply. Founding dealers receive a new key carrying their locked price. The location limit has to be enforced from inside `ee/`. The core supports exactly one location, and the code that creates and manages the second and later lots lives under the commercial license. A cap written into AGPL code would be one line that anyone may legally delete. Removing an ee check is just as easy technically, but it breaches the commercial license. Small dealers are unlikely to self-host at all, so stripped checks are a smaller revenue risk than simply not finding buyers. That conclusion is inferred, not measured.

### Free of charge must never mean free license

One rule ties the promotion to the repository. **Ee code must carry the commercial license from its first commit, even while the promotion makes it free.** Anything released under AGPL cannot be withdrawn, and only future releases can change license, as HashiCorp's relicense and the OpenTofu fork that followed show ([InfoQ](https://www.infoq.com/news/2023/08/hashicorp-adopts-bsl)). If BHPH servicing ships as AGPL "because it's free anyway", it stays free forever in every fork. The promotion should be a free key, never a free license.

The second rule is the **CLA before the first outside pull request**. While the founder owns every line, copyleft does not bind his own use, so he can dual-license and could even move ee code private later ([Heather Meeker](https://heathermeeker.com/2023/10/13/agpl-in-the-light-of-day/)). Once outside AGPL contributions land without a CLA, those contributors have licensed their code to him under AGPL only. Combining their code with proprietary ee code in a distributed build, a Tauri desktop app, an Android app or the network-served SaaS would then conflict with keeping ee closed. Karl Fogel notes that proprietary relicensing needs "the right to redistribute that contributor's code under a proprietary license" from every contributor ([Producing OSS](https://producingoss.com/en/proprietary-relicensing.html)). A DCO alone does not grant those rights ([Linux Foundation](https://bestpractices.linuxfoundation.org/ip/contribution-mechanisms-cla.html)). The CLA should cover both the core and `ee/`. The ee license templates already say the vendor retains rights to modifications ([Formbricks ee/LICENSE](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/formbricks/formbricks/main/apps/web/modules/ee/LICENSE)), but a CLA is cleaner. Mattermost and Grafana both require one ([Mattermost FAQ](https://docs.mattermost.com/product-overview/faq-mattermost-source-available-license.html); [Grafana](https://grafana.com/blog/2021/04/20/qa-with-our-ceo-on-relicensing/)). The cost is friction: Fogel reports that the asymmetry discourages outside contributions. A clearly drawn ee boundary softens that, because pull requests outside `ee/` remain ordinary open-source contributions, as GitLab and Metabase both state.

### What stays private

Keep private only what gains real protection from secrecy, which means server-side code that never ships to a client. That list is: the **license-signing private key** (never in any repo); **billing and subscription** code; **multi-tenant operations**, meaning infrastructure-as-code, secrets, backup and Object Lock configuration, and abuse and fraud heuristics; and **partner-restricted integrations** whose provider terms forbid publishing endpoints, credentials or logic. Credit bureaus, DMV electronic-reporting providers, licensed retail installment contract forms and paid valuation feeds are likely examples. Expose those as hosted APIs that self-hosted installs and the apps call with their key. Ee code compiled into the Tauri desktop or Android apps gains nothing from secrecy, since the JavaScript bundle can be inspected. Gate client features on server-issued entitlements instead, and consider MIT for SDKs and client packages, as Formbricks and Twenty do. Whether Google Play's terms are compatible with distributing an AGPL app was not researched and should be checked before the Android release.

## Conclusion

The three questions have one answer: put the founder's long-term promises where code licenses can enforce them. The always-free list (compliance, security, one location and export) is safest in AGPL code, because AGPL makes it irrevocable. Paid value is safest under a commercial ee license plus a signed key, because a key can expire on a published date and a license cannot be clawed back. The launch promotion is then just a key with an end date, ending in a downgrade rather than a lockout. That makes the promotion's ending a routine, verifiable event rather than a test of trust.

The market research changes the earlier framing in one important way. The competitive threat at the free end is no longer hypothetical: GARAGEBIRDS already gives away a hosted DMS without limits, and DealerVLO charges $29. The open-source DMS cannot win on price alone, and it should not try to by giving away hosting. It wins where incumbents are weakest: published prices for things nobody publishes (extra locations, pass-through fees), BHPH servicing that never cuts off a live loan, compliance depth that ships as auditable code, and an exit door that is always unlocked. The founding-dealer cohort is the real experiment. Its willingness to pay $49 for hosting and $99 for Pro is the missing data this research could not find, and it should settle the final price points before the paywall begins.
