# A lean compliance stack fits a small used car dealer

At a few cars a month, a small dealer can meet California's vehicle history rule and run its deals for well under $100 a month in software. The eROS/temporary-plate provider is a separate cost on top of that. **CVC 11713.26 requires a dated NMVTIS vehicle history report from a DOJ-approved data provider for every used car before it is displayed or offered for sale.** Price does not decide whether a report counts. Where it comes from does. That means **a free report from an approved provider would comply, and Carsforsale.com, an approved provider, advertises "100% FREE" NMVTIS-backed reports.** Its terms and report contents still need checking before you rely on it. The cheapest confirmed paid options are **Experian's $0.43 NMVTIS report** (it is unclear whether an AutoCheck subscription is required), **VINsmart at $4.95–$5.95 per report with no subscription**, **ClearVin at $279 a year for 35 reports**, and **VinAudit "NMVTIS Only" at $25 a month plus $0.50 per report**. Free tools such as NICB VINCheck, the NHTSA recall lookup and the vPIC decoder are useful checks, but none of them is an NMVTIS report. On the DMS side, established small-dealer systems cost **$75–$130 a month** (Wayne Reaves, AutoManager, DealerCenter, Carsforsale.com, Frazer). Newer cloud products start at **$0–$59**, and **DealerVLO ($29 a month) is the only vendor found that documents California CARS Act support** in detail. There is no usable free or open-source DMS that handles US or California compliance. A custom build can sensibly serve as an internal tracker only. It cannot replace the DMV-authorized eROS provider, purchased contract forms, or credit-bureau access. The recommended starting setup is a paid NMVTIS account plus the Carsforsale free report as a cross-check, a low-cost CARS Act–ready DMS, and a DMV-listed eROS provider. Get each vendor's CARS Act forms update and eROS integration confirmed in writing.

## California requires the NMVTIS report before a car goes on the lot

The rule works like a gate. Under CVC 11713.26(a), a dealer **"shall not display or offer for sale at retail a used vehicle"** without first getting an NMVTIS vehicle history report for that VIN from an NMVTIS data provider ([Justia, CVC 11713.26](https://law.justia.com/codes/california/2023/code-veh/division-5/chapter-4/article-1/section-11713-26)). The cost therefore follows the cars you acquire and display, not the cars you sell. A unit wholesaled after a week on the lot still needs a report. If the report shows junk or salvage history, an insurer total loss, or any title brand, subsection (b) requires a **red warning label at least 4" x 5½"**, with "WARNING" in 18-point bold and the body text in 14-point bold. It must be posted near the FTC Buyer's Guide for as long as the car is displayed, and the buyer gets a copy of the report on request before the sale. Subsection (c) waives the requirement when NMVTIS has no record and the dealer tried to get one. Subsection (f) protects the dealer from liability for errors in the report. Subsection (h) excludes RVs, motorcycles and off-highway vehicles ([Justia](https://law.justia.com/codes/california/2023/code-veh/division-5/chapter-4/article-1/section-11713-26)). The DOJ's California FAQ adds that **every title brand triggers the sticker, including a prior-rental brand** ([DOJ/BJA CA FAQ](https://vehiclehistory.bja.ojp.gov/sites/g/files/xyckuh261/files/media/document/caann_faqs.pdf)). The full statutory label wording was not captured in this research. Copy it from the statute, or use a provider's ready-made sticker such as VINData's ([VINData](https://vindata.com/dealers)).

The statute itself sets no retention period. DMV's operative notice, OLIN 2012-08, tells dealers to **keep a copy of the report in their files and not send it to DMV**, and says DMV "cannot assist dealers with obtaining an NMVTIS report" ([CA DMV OLIN 2012-08](https://www.vinsmart.com/media/1284/olin-2012-08-national-motor-vehicle-title-information-system-report.pdf)). Under the general dealer records rule, sales records must be kept for **3 years** ([13 CCR 272.00](https://regulations.justia.com/states/california/title-13/division-1/chapter-1/article-4-2/section-272-00)). So the safe approach is to save the dated PDF in each deal jacket for at least that long. VinAudit's suggested 4 years is a vendor recommendation, not a legal requirement ([VinAudit AB 1215 page](https://www.vinaudit.com/?p=24959)).

A standard Carfax or AutoCheck report is not the same product. NMVTIS is a narrow federal database with five indicators: title state and date, brand history, odometer, total-loss history and salvage history ([vehiclehistory.gov](https://vehiclehistory.bja.ojp.gov/nmvtis_understandingvhr)). Carfax and AutoCheck add accident, service and other data from their own sources. Experian sells NMVTIS as a separate report alongside AutoCheck ("one website for both, NMVTIS and AutoCheck") ([Experian](https://www.experian.com/automotive/automotive-autocheck-nmvtis-new-customer)). That strongly suggests a plain AutoCheck or Carfax report does not meet the 11713.26(d) definition, which requires the report date, the operator's disclaimer, and NMVTIS title, brand and odometer fields. No DMV statement on this point was found, so it is an inference. Order the vendor's NMVTIS-branded product. Carfax and AutoCheck remain optional marketing tools.

The free option is worth taking seriously, with one caution. The law tests where the report comes from, not what it cost. Subsection (e) also lets providers combine NMVTIS data with their own data ([Justia](https://law.justia.com/codes/california/2023/code-veh/division-5/chapter-4/article-1/section-11713-26)). So a dated report from Carsforsale.com, which lists itself as "an Approved NMVTIS Data Provider" and targets "consumers and sellers," **would appear to satisfy 11713.26 if it contains the subsection (d) elements** ([Carsforsale](https://www.carsforsale.com/free-vehicle-history-reports)). What remains unknown is whether its terms of use forbid commercial compliance use, and whether its report shows the date and the required NMVTIS disclaimer. Pull a sample report and read the terms before relying on it alone. The free report is most likely a lead-generation tool for Carsforsale's listing marketplace, which also sells a $99 DMS, so it could change without notice.

One cheap-looking option is a trap. Dealertrack's California registration sheet says the "costs of NMVTIS, CA INQ and temp tag paper [are] included in the transaction fee" ([Dealertrack RTS CA sheet](https://us-dev.dealertrack.com/wp-content/uploads/sites/2/2020/08/RTS_CAInfoSheet.pdf)). That check runs when the sale is registered, which is after the car has already been displayed and sold. It cannot satisfy the "before display" requirement unless the vendor confirms otherwise in writing. A better bundling lead is Motor Vehicle Software Corp, which appears on DMV's list of authorized eROS/temporary-plate providers ([CA DMV TLP Dealer FAQs](https://dmv.ca.gov/portal/file/temporary-license-plates-dealer-faqs-pdf)). Its products include DMVDesk, which the DOJ names as a dealer-only NMVTIS source, and the Vitu brand, which the DOJ lists as an approved commercial provider. The corporate relationship comes from prior knowledge and is not confirmed in the sources, so ask that vendor directly whether it bundles pre-display reports.

Free tools add useful information but cannot substitute for the report. **NICB VINCheck** flags insurer-reported theft and salvage, covering insurers with 92.49% of US premium. It allows **5 searches per IP address per day** and describes itself as "not a comprehensive vehicle history report" ([NICB](https://www.nicb.org/node/16)). The **NHTSA recall lookup** shows open safety recalls from the past 15 years ([NHTSA](https://vinrcl.safercar.gov/vin/)). The **vPIC API** decodes factory specs for free with no registration and no daily cap ([NHTSA vPIC FAQ](https://vpic.nhtsa.dot.gov/api/Home/Index/FAQ)). iSeeCars' free tier combines NICB and NHTSA data but puts title brands behind a paid plan ([iSeeCars](https://www.iseecars.com/vin-check)). Running all three free checks on each car at intake costs nothing. It catches recalls and theft flags that an NMVTIS-only report leaves out, and the NICB limit of 5 a day is no problem at this volume.

Two California requirements decide which system works, and the DMS handles neither one fully by itself. The first is **electronic Report of Sale and temporary plates (AB 516, in effect since January 1, 2019)**. Temporary plates can only be printed on special paper from a DMV-authorized provider: Fairfax Imaging, American Driving Records, AVRS, Dealertrack Registration and Titling, or Motor Vehicle Software Corp ([CA DMV TLP Dealer FAQs](https://dmv.ca.gov/portal/file/temporary-license-plates-dealer-faqs-pdf); [Dealertrack AB 516](https://us.dealertrack.com/?p=17941)). A small dealer needs one of those providers no matter which DMS it picks. What the DMS decides is whether deal data flows into that provider or has to be typed twice. No source confirmed which DMS products connect to which eROS provider. The second requirement is the **CARS Act (SB 766), in effect since October 1, 2026**. It repealed the paid 2-day contract cancellation option and replaced it with a **mandatory 3-day right to cancel on used cars priced at $50,000 or less**, which is lost if the car is driven more than 400 miles. Returns carry a restocking fee of 1.5% of the price, with a $200 minimum and a $600 maximum. The law also requires a standalone cancellation form, a notice on the first page of the contract, translated disclosures for deals negotiated mainly in Spanish, Chinese, Korean, Tagalog or Vietnamese, and 2-year retention of ads and disclosures ([California Dealer Academy](https://www.californiadealeracademy.com/articles/cars-act-sb-766-major-changes-coming-october-1-2026); [CA DMV OLIN 2026-10](https://www.dmv.ca.gov/portal/file/26olin10-pdf/)). DealerVLO is the only vendor in this research that documents each of these in detail. For DealerCenter, Frazer, Wayne Reaves, AutoManager and Carsforsale.com, **the date of their CARS Act forms update is unverified**. Any system still offering the old cancellation option in its F&I menu is a compliance risk.

Reviews of the two established leaders show predictable tradeoffs. DealerCenter users praise how easy it is to learn. They complain about lag, a slow mobile app on the lot, slow support, and **"hidden charges"** as modules and users are added ([Capterra DealerCenter](https://www.capterra.com/p/71935/DealerCenter/pricing/)). Frazer users like the all-in-one price that includes BHPH and accounting. They criticize the dated desktop interface and a support decline after a 2026 buyout ("This was a fantastic company before the buy out"), and one reviewer alleges payment-processing rates doubled after signing ([Capterra Frazer](https://www.capterra.com/p/34553/Frazer-Auto-Dealer-Software/reviews/)). No first-hand forum consensus from California small dealers was found.

## No free or open-source DMS handles California compliance

The open-source landscape is thin. SourceForge's "auto-dms" has been in "Planning" status since its last update in **May 2014** ([SourceForge](https://sourceforge.net/projects/auto-dms/)). A GitHub search on November 25, 2026 found 704 "car dealer management" repositories. The most-starred one had **88 stars and was last updated in 2022**. Most of the rest are student portfolio projects, many with no license, which means no legal right to reuse them, and none mentions Buyer's Guides, odometer disclosure, DMV reporting or US tax. The most capable open-source foundations are general ERP systems. Odoo has a $147.65 "Vehicle Dealership Management" module with VIN tracking, trade-ins and a finance calculator, but it does not cover titles, Buyer's Guides or sales tax, and its license is listed inconsistently ([Odoo Apps](https://apps.odoo.com/apps/modules/19.0/vehicle_dealership)). ERPNext has only a forum workaround that treats each car as an inventory item tracked by VIN ([Frappe forum](https://discuss.frappe.io/t/set-up-a-car-dealership-with-erpnext/95978)).

"Free" commercial software is mostly trials or capped trackers. LotPulse is free up to 10 vehicles, CarVadia has a free tier with a vehicle cap, and Car Dealer Tracker offers a free "2-vehicle garage" ([SourceForge free list](https://sourceforge.net/software/auto-dealer/free-version/)). None of them claims to generate California contracts, Buyer's Guides or reports of sale. GARAGEBIRDS' free plan is the one exception that calls itself a DMS, but its California features are undocumented. The practical cost floor for a California lot is therefore set by the eROS provider and the forms, not by the software.

## A custom build works only as a tracker next to purchased pieces

Some pieces of the work cannot be done in-house. Temporary plates and the electronic report of sale must go through a DMV-listed provider. Credit pulls need a bureau or reseller relationship. Retail installment contracts are normally bought from forms suppliers or provided by lenders, because drafting them yourself carries legal risk. What a custom system can do well is act as the record system for each deal. VIN decoding is free through the vPIC API, which needs no key and decodes up to 50 VINs per batch request ([NHTSA vPIC API](https://vpic.nhtsa.dot.gov/api/)). The Buyer's Guide can be filled from the **FTC's official fillable form**. Dealers who print their own must match the Rule's exact wording, type and layout, with no logos, in black ink on white paper at least 11" x 7¼". The Spanish version is required when the sale is conducted in Spanish ([FTC Dealer's Guide](https://ftc.gov/business-guidance/resources/dealers-guide-used-car-rule); [FTC fillable form](https://www.ftc.gov/business-guidance/resources/buyers-guide-fillable-form)). Sales tax needs a dated rate table, because CDTFA rates change quarterly. As of October 1, 2026, **neighboring cities in the same county can differ by a full point (for example 8.75% in some cities and 7.75% in the unincorporated county)** ([CDTFA rates](https://cdtfa.ca.gov/taxes-and-fees/rates.aspx?os=os)). Records must be kept 3 years for DMV purposes and 2 years for CARS Act purposes. Electronic copies must be **"non-alterable"** and protected by access controls ([13 CCR 272.02](https://www.law.cornell.edu/regulations/california/13-CCR-272.02)). A minimum data model has four parts. Vehicle records hold the VIN, decode, cost and reconditioning, title status and a reference to the NMVTIS report. Deal records hold the price breakdown, tax, fees, add-on consents, the CARS Act cancellation window, the Buyer's Guide version, the odometer statement and the eROS/temporary plate number. The other two parts are an index of scanned signed documents and a log of ads and communications. No source gave real build times, so these are estimates. A no-code version in Airtable or Google Sheets with Apps Script would take roughly **20–60 hours**. A proper web app with login security (MFA) and encrypted storage would take **100–300+ hours**, plus ongoing upkeep for tax rates and law changes like the CARS Act.

The biggest DIY risk comes from data security, not from the code. If the dealer arranges financing, it falls under the **FTC Safeguards Rule**. That requires a written security program, a designated Qualified Individual, a risk assessment, encryption, MFA for everyone, monitoring or periodic testing, and FTC notification within 30 days of a breach affecting 500 or more consumers. Cash-only sales are generally outside the rule ([FTC Safeguards FAQ for Auto Dealers](https://www.ftc.gov/system/files/ftc_gov/pdf/2025-GLB-Safeguards-FAQs-for-Auto-dealers.pdf)). Whoever builds and runs a home-made system holding financing data effectively becomes the Qualified Individual. Social Security numbers and credit reports should never sit in a spreadsheet. Whether the rule's small-institution exemptions apply was not confirmed. The economics also cut against a full build: a commercial DMS with documented CARS Act forms costs $29 a month, so a custom system saves almost no money while taking on all the forms and tax risk.

## Recommended setup for a few cars a month

| Option | Monthly software cost (excluding eROS provider) | Compliance coverage | Best for |
|---|---|---|---|
| A. Lean commercial (recommended start) | ~$30–$60 (DealerVLO $29 + NMVTIS ~$2–$27) | CARS Act forms documented; separate eROS provider; finance contracts from lender or forms supplier | Cash and outside-financed deals at 2–6 cars/month |
| B. Established all-in-one | ~$100–$155 (DealerCenter $99 or Frazer $129 + NMVTIS) | Broad forms libraries and lender links; CARS Act update must be confirmed | Planning BHPH or growth within a year |
| C. DIY hybrid | ~$0–$30 + NMVTIS + 20–300 hours | Only what you build; all forms and tax risk is yours | Internal analytics or learning, not primary compliance |

For NMVTIS, call Experian first (1-888-409-2204) and ask whether the $0.43 report requires a paid AutoCheck plan. If it does not, that is the cheapest confirmed compliant source. If it does, open a **VINsmart** account, which has no subscription, $4.95–$5.95 per report, and 5 free credits after verification. A **ClearVin annual plan** ($279 for 35 reports) also works well at about 3 cars a month. Meanwhile, pull a sample **Carsforsale.com** free report and read its terms. If it shows the report date and the NMVTIS disclaimer and allows dealer use, it can serve as a backup or even the main source. Until then, use it only as a cross-check. Save every dated report PDF in the deal jacket, keep a stock of red warning stickers, and run NICB, the NHTSA recall lookup and vPIC on each car at intake. For the DMS, start a **DealerVLO trial**. Check that it prints the Buyer's Guide in the FTC format and connects to your chosen eROS provider. If it falls short, or if in-house financing is planned, move to **DealerCenter** (add the $50 BHPH module later) or **Frazer** (BHPH included). Before choosing any vendor, get four things in writing: total cost including add-ons and per-deal fees, contract and cancellation terms, the date of its CARS Act forms update, and which eROS providers it integrates with. When choosing the eROS provider, ask Motor Vehicle Software Corp, Dealertrack and AVRS whether they bundle a pre-display NMVTIS report. eROS pricing was not covered in this research.

## Conclusion

For a startup lot, the real compliance spending goes to the DMV-authorized eROS provider and current forms, not to the history report or the DMS. The NMVTIS rule can cost almost nothing, and software prices have fallen far enough that a home-made DMS no longer saves meaningful money. So the decisions that matter most are about process: pull the report when a car is acquired rather than when it sells, and make sure every form in use reflects the CARS Act changes of October 1, 2026. The statute focuses on whether a report exists and comes from an approved source, so a dealer who saves a dated report for every VIN is protected even when the report has errors.

The main open questions can all be settled with a few phone calls. Can Carsforsale's free report be used for dealer compliance? Does Experian's $0.43 rate require an AutoCheck subscription? Which mainstream DMS vendors have shipped CARS Act forms? Does the dealer's eROS provider bundle NMVTIS reports? DMV's most recent NMVTIS guidance dates from 2012, and the statute text here comes from Justia's 2023 version, not the official 2026 code. Check the current text on leginfo before printing the warning label, and plan to recheck the DOJ provider list each year. A custom build is most useful as a later project: a dashboard on top of DMS exports that tracks cost per car, days on lot, and retention deadlines.
